Lesson six. In-plane behavior. Panels as the building's lateral system. So far we've pushed on the face of the panel. Now turn it ninety degrees. The same panels that bend under wind on their face are also, edge-on, the shear walls that hold the whole building up against earthquakes and wind.

Remember the load path from lesson two: wind hits the long walls, they lean on the roof diaphragm, the diaphragm carries the force sideways like a giant flat beam, and delivers it to the end walls. Those end walls now feel force along their length, in their strong direction, and they resist it as squat cantilevers sticking up from the ground.

First, vocabulary that interviewers check. In the seismic code's taxonomy, tilt-up panels are classified as intermediate precast structural walls. Yes, precast, even though they're cast on site; tilt-up is site-cast precast in code language. They typically get a response factor of four as bearing walls. And the design philosophy for intermediate precast walls is all about the connections: any yielding in an earthquake is supposed to happen in the connections, which must either be ductile enough to yield at one and a half times their yield strength, or be protected by making them stronger than the forces that can ever reach them. The connections are the fuses. Design them like fuses.

Now the checks. Shear itself, force divided among the panels along the line, almost never governs on solid panels. Concrete walls are shear monsters. The real fight is overturning. Picture a single panel, twenty-four feet wide, being pushed sideways along its length at the roof line. It wants to tip over about its bottom corner. What holds it down? Its own weight, and in the seismic combination you only get to count ninety percent of that. A wide, solid panel wins this fight easily. A narrow panel loses. And what did we say data center walls are full of? Louvers. Openings. Narrow legs.

So here's the data center problem in one picture: a side wall that's forty percent openings. The remaining legs are doing double duty. Face-on, each leg carries doubled wind because it picks up the opening's share. Edge-on, those same skinny legs are now your only shear walls on the line, and they have almost no self-weight to prevent tipping. The fixes, and you should be able to list these in an interview: add hold-downs, steel tension anchors tying the panel legs down into the footings. Or couple the legs, connect them across the headers so a group of legs tips as one wide unit instead of individually. Or steal the shear, use drag struts along the roof to carry the line's force past the holey wall to a solid panel that can handle it. Or just thicken those panels. Usually it's some combination.

Two more things. Sliding: the panel base has to transfer the shear into the slab and footing, through dowels and the closure strip. Easy, but somebody has to draw it. And the hybrid question. Big AI data halls increasingly put a steel braced frame inside the building to carry the lateral loads, with tilt-up panels just as cladding. That changes everything about the connections: now the panels have to ride along while the steel frame sways in an earthquake, without being pried apart. Slotted, flexible connections. So on any tilt-up review, your first question is: who takes the lateral? The panels, or a frame? Every connection detail follows from that answer.

Takeaways. Panels are intermediate precast shear walls; connections are the designed fuses. Overturning of narrow legs, not shear, is the real fight, and hold-downs, coupling, and drag struts are the medicine. And always ask first: who takes the lateral?
