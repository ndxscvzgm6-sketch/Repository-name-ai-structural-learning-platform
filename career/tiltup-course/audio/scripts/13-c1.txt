Meet panel P seven, a solid interior panel from a data hall. Twenty-four feet wide. Thirty-six feet tall. Nine and a quarter inches thick. It spans thirty-two feet clear from the footing to the roof line, and carries a four foot parapet above. Concrete at four thousand p s i, steel at sixty. Our trial reinforcement: number five bars at twelve inches, a single curtain in the center of the wall, which puts the effective depth at half the thickness, about four and five eighths inches. The roof brings sixteen pounds dead and twenty pounds roof live per square foot, from joists spanning twenty-five feet, landing on seats about eight inches off the panel centerline. The site gives us twenty-six pounds per square foot of component wind, and a moderate seismic acceleration of a half.

First, self weight. Nine and a quarter inches of concrete weighs about a hundred and sixteen pounds per square foot of wall. The weight above mid-height, sixteen feet of wall plus that four foot parapet, twenty feet total, comes to about twenty-three hundred pounds per foot of width. Remember that number, it's the silent driver of everything.

Second, who governs the panel's bending, wind or earthquake? The seismic body force is forty percent of the acceleration times the wall weight: that's about twenty-three pounds per square foot. Wind is twenty-six. Wind governs, but barely. On a higher seismic site, this flips, always check both.

Third, the roof connection force, and notice how different it is. The roof spans two hundred feet of flexible metal deck, so the amplification factor maxes out at two. Forty percent of the acceleration, times two, times the twenty-three hundred pounds of wall hanging on each foot of connection: roughly nine hundred twenty-five pounds per foot. Compare that to the twenty-three pounds per square foot body force. The connection force is the number that would tear the roof off. File it, we design that connection separately.

Fourth, the governing combination for the panel: full dead, full wind, half roof live. The axial load at mid-height comes to about three and a half kips per foot of width. Quick entry check: spread over the section, that's thirty-two p s i of axial stress, way below the six percent of concrete strength cap of two hundred forty. We're flexure-dominated, the slender wall method applies.

Fifth, the first-order moment at mid-height. Wind: twenty-six pounds over a thirty-two foot simple span gives about three point three kip feet, call it forty kip inches per foot. Add the eccentric roof moment: the factored roof reaction, about seven hundred thirty pounds, times its eight inch eccentricity, half of which shows up at mid-height, about three kip inches. Total: forty-three kip inches per foot of width. That's the moment before amplification. And from lesson four, you already suspect the truth: the real moment will be half again bigger.
