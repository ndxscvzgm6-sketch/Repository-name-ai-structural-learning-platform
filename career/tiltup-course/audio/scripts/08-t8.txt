Lesson eight. Connections, and the roof anchorage story. If you only master one lesson in this course, make it this one. It has the best story, the most important code provisions, and the highest interview value.

Here's the truth about tilt-up buildings in earthquakes: the panels almost never fail. The connections fail. February ninth, nineteen seventy-one, the San Fernando earthquake. And then February seventeenth, nineteen ninety-four, Northridge. In both quakes, hundreds of tilt-up and masonry big-box buildings failed the same way, like a script. The flexible metal deck roof whipped back and forth. The connection between the wall and the roof tore out. The heavy wall panel fell outward. And the roof, having lost its support, came down after it.

Why did the connections tear out? Three villains. First, in older buildings, the roof framing sat on wood ledgers bolted to the wall, and the earthquake bent those ledgers across the grain, the weakest possible way to load wood. It snaps like a cracker. Cross-grain bending is now banned. Second, some walls were connected only to the thin metal deck, and the deck attachments unzipped. Third, and most fundamental, nobody appreciated how violently a flexible diaphragm amplifies the forces at its ends. The roof is a diving board, and the wall connection is holding the diving board's tip.

Out of those failures came the modern anchorage rules, and here's the design chain you need to know cold. Step one, the force. The wall-to-roof anchorage force is forty percent of the short period acceleration, times an amplification factor, times the weight of wall hanging on the connection, that's the top half of the wall plus the whole parapet. The amplification factor grows with the roof span and maxes out at two. Two hundred foot roof span? Your connection force just doubled. Step two: anchor the panel to the roof framing, the joists and beams, never to the deck alone. The deck is never allowed to be the tension link. Step three: once the force is in the framing, it has to travel deeper into the roof. You design little local diaphragm regions called sub-diaphragms that gather the anchor forces and feed them into big continuous cross-ties. Step four, and this amazes people: those cross-ties must run continuously across the entire building, from one wall all the way to the opposite wall, with every single splice along the way designed for the amplified force. The building gets sewn together, wall to wall, in both directions.

Beyond the roof line, know the rest of the connection family. Panel to footing: bearing on shims, grouted, with dowels. Panel to slab: dowels into that closure strip pour. Panel to panel: weld plates at the chord lines, and here's a trap, don't let anyone weld panels into one long rigid chain, because concrete shrinks, and restrained shrinkage cracks panels. Connect at designed points with ductile details. Roof gravity: joist seats on embedded plates, and check that embed for the combination of gravity plus that anchorage tension, same piece of steel, two jobs. And in hybrid buildings with steel frames, panel connections must let the frame drift in an earthquake without prying the panels. Slotted holes, flexible straps.

Now, here's your sixty second interview set piece. Practice saying this out loud. Tilt-up's seismic history is a connection story. Seventy-one and ninety-four tore roofs off because flexible diaphragms amplified the wall anchorage forces, and the connections, wood ledgers in cross-grain bending, deck-only attachments, couldn't take it. Today the code makes us design anchorage at up to double the wall force, forbids deck tension paths, and requires sub-diaphragms feeding continuous cross-ties that run the full width of the building. So when I review a tilt-up package, I start at the roof line: anchorage forces, tie continuity, then panel flexure. Because that's the order the earthquake checks them in. Deliver that, calmly, and you will sound like twenty years of experience. Next: lifting.
