Lesson three. Loads on a panel. This is where beginners lose points, and where reviewers catch real errors in real packages. The math in the next two lessons is mechanical once the loads are right. So let's get them right.

First, wind. Here's the classic rookie mistake. Building codes give you two different kinds of wind pressure. There's the pressure used to design the building's overall lateral system, called main wind force resisting system pressures. And there's a higher pressure used to design individual components, called components and cladding pressures. A wall panel bending under wind is a component. It gets the higher pressure. Period. If you see a panel calc using the lower system-level wind, that calc is unconservative, and you've just caught something real. On top of that, panels near building corners see even higher suction, and those tall parapets and screen walls get amplified pressures hitting them from both sides.

Second, earthquake. And here I need you to burn one distinction into your memory, because it is the single most important code fact in this entire course. There are two different seismic forces, for two different jobs. Force number one designs the panel itself, its own bending. It's forty percent of the short period acceleration, times the panel weight. Force number two designs the connection between the wall and the roof. And it can be up to two times bigger. Why? Because flexible metal deck roofs whip back and forth in an earthquake like a diving board, and they yank on the wall connections much harder than the ground shakes the wall itself. The code captures this with an amplification factor that grows with the roof span, maxing out at double for spans of two hundred feet or more. This amplification is written in the blood of those nineteen seventy-one and nineteen ninety-four roof collapses. Design the panel for the body force. Design the connection for the amplified anchorage force. Two different numbers. Always.

Third, gravity. The forces are small, but the eccentricities are big. The roof joists sit on seats that stick out from the face of the panel, so the roof load lands maybe eight inches off the panel's centerline. Small load, real lever arm, genuine moment at the top of the panel. Then there's the panel's own weight. For bending at mid-height, what matters is everything above the middle: the top half of the wall, plus that entire parapet. Here's why the parapet is sneaky: it's pure weight, sitting high up on a wall that's about to bow sideways under wind. When the wall bows, that weight rides the bow and pushes it further. The parapet is a free moment generator, and forgetting it is error number three on my list of classics.

Before you run the slender wall method, the code makes you check one entry condition: the axial stress at mid-height has to be tiny, no more than six percent of the concrete strength. Tilt-up panels are bending members, not columns. If your wall is carrying serious axial load, this method doesn't apply.

Which load combination governs? Usually the one with full dead load, full wind, and half the roof live load, because more axial load means more of that bowing amplification. But always check the light combination too, with only ninety percent of dead load, and check earthquake against wind. And there's one more check that often surprises people by governing the whole design: the service level deflection limit. Under everyday service wind, the panel's sideways bow can't exceed its height divided by one hundred fifty. For a thirty-two foot panel, that's about two and a half inches.

So, your three classic errors to hunt for, whether in your own work or someone else's. One: system-level wind used instead of component wind. Two: the amplified anchorage force missing, with the roof connection designed for the little body force. Three: the parapet forgotten, its weight, its two-sided wind, and its own cantilever seismic design. Find one of those in a review, and you've earned your seat at the table.
