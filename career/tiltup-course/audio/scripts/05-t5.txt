Lesson five. The slender wall method, part two. The working sequence. This is the longest lesson, and the most valuable. I'll walk the exact calculation order you'll use in the capstone, in plain spoken language. Don't worry about writing anything down while you're listening; the platform has every equation printed, and the capstone repeats them all with numbers. Just get the logic into your bones.

Step one, materials. From the concrete strength you get the elastic modulus, and from that, the modular ratio, the stiffness of steel compared to concrete, usually around eight, and the code says never take it less than six. You also compute the cracking moment: the tension strength of plain concrete, which is seven and a half times the square root of the concrete strength, times the section modulus of the gross section.

Step two, and here's the detail that most hand calcs get wrong. The axial load actually helps the wall's bending strength, because it pre-compresses the section. The code folds that benefit into something called the effective steel area. You take the actual rebar area, and you add the axial load divided by the steel yield stress. For steel in the center of the wall, it's that simple. This effective steel area gets used everywhere downstream, in the strength, and in the cracked stiffness. And notice: different load combinations have different axial loads, so the effective steel, and therefore the capacity, changes from combination to combination. That trips people up constantly.

Step three, capacity. With the effective steel area you run a completely standard flexural calculation: depth of the stress block, neutral axis, check that the steel strain exceeds three quarters of a percent so you're ductile, and compute the moment capacity with a strength reduction factor of nine tenths. Then compute the cracked moment of inertia. Expect a startlingly small number, five to eight percent of the gross value. That's not an error. That's the whole point.

Step four, the strength check. First-order moment: wind moment at mid-height, plus half of the eccentric roof moment. Then the magnifier: one divided by one minus the axial load times the height squared, over three quarters of forty-eight times the concrete modulus times the cracked inertia. Multiply, and compare against the capacity. If that denominator is getting small, or heaven forbid goes negative, the wall is unstable on paper. Thicken it.

Step five, serviceability. Under service wind, compute the deflection using the code's two-part rule: below two thirds of the cracking moment, the wall behaves like an uncracked section; above that, you interpolate along the cracked branch. And here's the trap: the service moment itself includes P delta, the axial load times the service deflection. But the deflection depends on the moment. Circular? Yes. So you iterate. Guess a deflection, compute the moment, recompute the deflection. It converges in two or three rounds. Then check against height over one hundred fifty.

Let me also give you the reviewer's feel for healthy numbers, because owner side engineers audit more calcs than they produce. For a thirty-ish foot panel, nine and a quarter inches thick, with number five bars at twelve inches: cracked inertia around forty to sixty in the standard units, against roughly eight hundred gross. Magnifier one point four to one point eight. Strength utilization seventy to ninety-five percent. Service deflection a small fraction of the limit. And one more audit gem: on lightly reinforced panels, the requirement that flexural strength must exceed the cracking moment is often the check that actually governs. Number four bars at twelve fail it where number five bars barely pass. When a designer proudly shows you a thin panel with minimum steel, that's the first place to look.

Recap of the sequence, say it with me. Materials and cracking moment. Effective steel area, per combination. Capacity, ductility check, cracked inertia. Magnified moment against capacity. Iterated service deflection against height over one fifty. Five steps. In the capstone you'll run every one of them with real numbers.
