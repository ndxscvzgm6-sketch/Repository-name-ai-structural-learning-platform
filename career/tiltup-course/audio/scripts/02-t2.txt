Lesson two. The anatomy of a tilt-up building. Before we can design anything, you need to be able to walk through one of these buildings in your head and name every part. Design reviews are anatomy tests before they're math tests.

Start with the panel itself. It's a reinforced concrete plate, usually with one curtain of vertical and horizontal rebar right in the middle of the thickness, sometimes two curtains for thicker panels. For gravity and wind, each panel spans vertically, like a tall skinny beam standing from the footing up to the roof. Side to side, the panels stand shoulder to shoulder with small gaps between them, about three quarters of an inch, filled with flexible sealant. And here's something that surprises people: those neighboring panels are basically independent. Each panel stands on its own for wind. They only get connected to each other at specific engineered points, usually up at the roof line.

Now the openings. Data centers are full of them. Dock doors, personnel doors, and especially louvers, those big grilles that let cooling air in. When you cut a hole in a panel, what's left gets names. The vertical strips beside the opening are the legs. The band of concrete over the top is the header. The legs work extra hard, they carry everything the opening displaced, and we'll spend a whole lesson on them.

Above the roof, data center panels keep going. That extension is the parapet, or when it gets really tall, six to fifteen feet, we call it a screen wall, because its job is to hide the rooftop chillers and air handlers. Remember that parapet. It shows up in the math later, and it's sneaky.

Cast into the panel are pieces of hardware. Lifting inserts, where the crane connects. Brace inserts, where the temporary pipe braces attach. And structural embeds, steel plates that the roof joists will sit on, called joist seats, plus plates for connecting panel to panel. Here's an important professional detail: the lifting inserts are not located by the building's design engineer. A specialty lifting engineer, working for the contractor, designs the rigging and picks those locations. Knowing that division of responsibility marks you as someone who understands how this industry actually works.

Down at the bottom, panels bear on continuous footings, sitting on small stacks of shims, and get grouted solid. There's usually a strip of floor slab near the perimeter that's left unpoured until after the panels are up. That's the closure strip, and it gets poured back to lock the panel bases to the floor.

Up top is the roof: typically steel joists and joist girders with a corrugated metal deck. And this is the most important concept in this lesson. That metal deck roof is what engineers call a flexible diaphragm. When the wind blows on the walls, or an earthquake shakes them, the walls lean on the roof, and the roof acts like a big flat beam, collecting all those forces and dragging them to the side walls, which resist them as shear walls. The complete chain goes like this: wind hits a panel, the panel spans vertically and leans on the roof, the roof diaphragm carries the force sideways to the end walls, those end walls take it down to their footings. Every link in that chain is a connection somebody has to design. And until the roof exists, the temporary braces are doing the diaphragm's job. That's why removing braces too early is a life safety decision, not a convenience decision.

Let me close with what makes a data center different from a warehouse, because you should be able to rattle this off. Taller panels, forty-five to sixty feet with screens. Walls shredded by huge louver banks. Much heavier stuff hanging from the roof, electrical busway, cable trays, piping, ten to fifty pounds per square foot where a warehouse might have five. Sometimes insulated sandwich panels, two layers of concrete with foam in between, for energy codes. And tilt-up used even for interior walls between data halls.

Your takeaways. One: panels span vertically and stand independently. Two: the roof diaphragm is the middle link of the whole lateral system, and braces substitute for it during construction. Three: legs, headers, parapets, and embeds are where the real engineering lives. Next up: the loads.
