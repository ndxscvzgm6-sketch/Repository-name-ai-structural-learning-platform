Capstone, part B. The slender wall calculation. This is the heart of the artifact. I'll walk it as a story with numbers; the platform prints every equation. Listen for the logic and the sanity checks.

Step one, constants. Four thousand p s i concrete gives an elastic modulus around three point six million p s i, and a modular ratio, steel to concrete, of about eight. The cracking moment for our twelve inch strip works out to about eighty-one kip inches. Also note the gross moment of inertia: about seven hundred ninety in the standard units. Watch what happens to that number when the section cracks.

Step two, effective steel. Our actual rebar is point three one square inches per foot. Now add the axial load's contribution: three and a half kips divided by sixty k s i of yield gives about point zero six more. Effective steel: point three seven square inches. Six percent might sound small, but on lightly reinforced panels this term decides pass or fail, and forgetting it is the single most common hand-calc error.

Step three, capacity. Run the standard flexure numbers and you get a neutral axis around six tenths of an inch deep, a steel strain of nearly two percent, more than twice the ductility gate, and a design moment capacity of about eighty-seven kip inches. Pause on a subtle check: capacity, eighty-seven, versus cracking moment, eighty-one. We pass the brittleness check, but barely! Drop to number four bars and this panel fails, not in strength, but by being too lightly reinforced to survive its own first crack. On thin panels, that check governs more often than anyone expects, and knowing that is reviewer gold.

Step four, the cracked stiffness. Transformed steel plus the little concrete compression block gives a cracked moment of inertia of about forty-eight. Against seven hundred ninety gross. Six percent. Sixteen times softer than the gross section. Exactly the range lesson five promised.

Step five, the magnifier. Axial load, height squared, against the reduced cracked stiffness: the ratio comes out around point four one, and the magnifier is one over one minus that: one point seven. So the design moment is one point seven times forty-three: about seventy-three kip inches, against a capacity of eighty-seven. Utilization: eighty-four percent. The panel works, with honest margins. And absorb the headline: the second-order effect added seventy percent to the moment. A calc using gross stiffness would have proudly reported fifty percent utilization and been wrong by the entire P delta share.

Step six, service deflection. Service wind, about sixty percent of ultimate, gives a service moment around twenty-six kip inches, well below two thirds of cracking, so we're on the uncracked branch. Iterate the P delta: converges in two rounds to about point one four inches of bow. The limit is height over one fifty: two point five six inches. We're at five percent of the limit. Comfortable.

So panel P seven, nine and a quarter inches, number fives at twelve: good. Strength eighty-four percent, deflection trivial, brittleness check thin but passing. And now you own two interview sentences. First: on my baseline panel the magnifier was one point seven, I watch utilization in the denominator, not the numerator. Second: lightly reinforced panels often fail the cracking-moment check before strength, that's the first thing I audit on thin panels.

Your homework before part C: run the same panel at the corner wind zone, thirty-four pounds per square foot. You'll find it fails, around a hundred and ten percent, and you get to choose the fix: tighter bar spacing, or a thicker panel. Showing a failure and your fix demonstrates judgment, which is worth more than a clean pass. Part C stands the panel up.
