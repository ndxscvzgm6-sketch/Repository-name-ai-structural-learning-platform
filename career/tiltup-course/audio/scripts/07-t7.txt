Lesson seven. Openings, legs, and headers. Solid panel design is arithmetic. Openings are engineering. And since data center walls are full of openings, this is where your bread gets buttered.

The method is old-fashioned and completely reliable: design strips. When a panel has an opening, you slice it, in your mind, into vertical strips. The full-height strips beside the opening are the legs. The horizontal band over the opening is the header. Then you make each strip carry its fair share, and you trace every load explicitly.

Start with the header. It spans horizontally, from leg to leg, over the opening. It carries its own weight, the wind pressure on its own face, and, watch for this, anything bearing on it from above. If a roof joist lands on the panel over a doorway, that load goes into the header. The header delivers all of this as reactions onto the legs at each end.

Now the legs, where everything accumulates. A leg carries its own tributary wind, roughly half the opening's width of extra wind on each side. Plus the header's reactions, both the sideways wind reaction and the gravity. Plus its concentrated share of everything above: the roof, the parapet. So compared to a strip of solid wall, a leg beside a big louver bank might carry two to three times the demand, on a reduced width. And each leg is still a slender wall! It gets the full treatment from lesson five: effective steel, cracked inertia, magnifier, service deflection.

Here's why legs are where designs actually fail. Remember the magnifier: it explodes as axial load grows and stiffness shrinks. Legs have more axial load, same height, and often less section. The magnifier climbs fastest exactly where you have the least concrete. Panels rarely fail solid. Calculation packages fail at legs.

Three traps to hunt in every review. Trap one: architectural reveals. Those decorative grooves cast into the face. A one inch reveal crossing a seven and a quarter inch leg just removed fourteen percent of the section, at the worst possible place. Always check the thickness at the reveal line. Trap two: stacked or offset openings, a louver over a door. The load path zigzags, and you have to trace it, not average it. Trap three: claiming too much width. The code limits how much panel a leg can claim on each side of an opening, roughly twelve times the thickness, and neighboring legs can't claim the same concrete twice.

Let me leave you with the picture to sketch from memory before any data center interview, because it's the single most likely whiteboard scenario. An intake wall with a continuous eight foot tall louver band running across it, and a tall screen parapet above. What you've really got is a table: two legs, holding up a deep header, carrying a wall and screen above the louvers. That header is now a transfer girder for the parapet weight and the screen's wind. The legs are mini-columns collecting everything. And edge-on, the whole thing works like a portal frame. If you can talk through that load path smoothly, top to bottom, you sound like you've built one.

Takeaways. Slice into strips, make the header a beam, pile the header's reactions plus concentrated axial onto the legs, run each leg as a full slender wall at its thinnest real section. Watch reveals, stacked openings, and greedy width claims. Next lesson is the most important one in the course: connections, and the roof anchorage story.
