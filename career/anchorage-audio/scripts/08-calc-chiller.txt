Lecture eight. Worked calculation two: the rooftop chiller. Same discipline, new villain: height. Everything that was comfortable on grade becomes a fight on the roof.

The setup. An air-cooled chiller on the roof of a single-story data hall. Operating weight twenty-four thousand pounds, twelve tons, and note the discipline: shipping weight is nineteen thousand, we design gravity for operating, but check uplift and overturning with the empty machine, both cases, always. Footprint: twenty-two feet by seven and a half feet, eight anchor points into steel dunnage beams on the roof structure. Center of gravity forty-two inches up. Site: same, S D S one point oh, importance one point five. No isolators this time, the unit sits on neoprene pads within a rated curb-rail assembly, rigid path.

Step one, the force, and here's the lesson of the lecture. The unit is at roof level: z equals h, the height amplifier is one plus two: three. Rigid-ish mechanical unit: amplification one point oh, response two point five. F p: zero point four times one, times three, times one point five, divided by two point five: seventy-two percent of weight. Check against the cap: two point four times weight, fine. So: zero point seven two times twenty-four thousand: seventeen thousand three hundred pounds lateral. On grade this same machine would see twenty-nine percent, the minimum floor, actually governs there: thirty percent of S D S I p, forty-five percent... hold that thought: the minimum, zero point three times one point oh times one point five, is forty-five percent of weight, so on grade this chiller designs for forty-five, on the roof, seventy-two. Height cost us sixty percent more force. When owners debate roof versus yard plant, this is a real line item.

Step two, overturning, the short direction again. Moment: seventeen point three kips at forty-two inches: seven hundred twenty-seven kip-inches. Resisting, and use the empty machine for the uplift check: nineteen kips, times the point-nine-minus-vertical-earthquake factor, about thirteen point three kips effective, times half of ninety inches: about six hundred kip-inches. The moment wins: net uplift on the windward anchor row. Four anchors that side: tension about a kip and a half each after the algebra, plus two point two kips shear each. Modest numbers, but genuinely in tension now, no free ride from gravity.

Step three, the attachment world changes on a roof. These anchors don't go into concrete: they're bolts into the dunnage steel, so the checks are steel-to-steel: bolt shear and tension with interaction, the curb rail's local bending, and the welds of the dunnage to the roof framing. But the chapter-seventeen thinking still applies at the other end of the load path: the dunnage connects to the building, and somewhere, the force enters the diaphragm and finds the lateral system. Walk it: unit to rail, rail to dunnage, dunnage to joists or beams, beams to connections, into the deck's braced lines. And the two structural coordination items nobody owns unless you do: the joists under that dunnage were designed for these point loads, by the joist supplier, per your loading diagram, verify, and the deck is never the tension link for any hold-down strap.

Step four, the honest extras. Wind: on a roof, wind on a big-box chiller competes with seismic, run both, envelope the anchors. Vibration: neoprene pads plus operating machine near a hall with vibration limits: check the isolation efficiency against the sensitive-equipment criteria, and baseline at commissioning. And the displacement demand: the chilled-water piping crossing from the roof onto the unit needs flexible connections rated for the relative movement, rigid pipe to a rocking machine is a shear-off waiting for its earthquake.

The pattern you should be internalizing: force is arithmetic; the engineering is the load path, the governing edge cases, and the coordination seams. One calc to go, the one that overturns: the battery rack.
