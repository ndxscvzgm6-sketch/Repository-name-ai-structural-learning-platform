Lecture nine. Worked calculation three: the lithium battery rack, and the closing of the series. This is the purest overturning problem in the building, and the one with a fire code looking over your shoulder.

The setup. A lithium-ion U P S battery cabinet line: each cabinet seven feet tall, thirty inches wide, forty inches deep, twenty-six hundred pounds loaded. Cabinets ganged in rows of eight, bolted side to side, in a dedicated battery room, risk category four, importance one point five, obviously, S D S one point oh. Center of gravity: forty inches up, the cells stack low, thankfully. Anchorage: four floor anchors per cabinet into the slab, plus the code overlay: the energy storage standard governs room design, spacing, and demands engineered anchorage explicitly.

Step one, the force. In-building, ground floor: height amplifier one. Battery racks on the classic tables: amplification one point oh, response two point five, some configurations argue two point five on amplification if rack-like and flexible, and here's the professional move: check the vendor's seismic certification first. Quality cabinet lines carry shake-table certification to the rack standard with published anchorage reactions. If certified reactions exist, your job is receiving them; if not, you compute: zero point four, times one, times one point five, over two point five: twenty-four percent, below the thirty-percent-of-S-D-S-I-p floor, so the minimum governs: forty-five percent of weight. Per cabinet: zero point four five times twenty-six hundred: eleven hundred seventy pounds lateral, at forty inches.

Step two, overturning, cross-aisle direction, the fifteen-inch half-depth is the whole story. Moment per cabinet: one point one seven kips times forty: forty-seven kip-inches. Restoring: weight, reduced: two point six times about point seven effective: one point eight kips, times fifteen inches: twenty-seven kip-inches. The moment wins decisively. Net uplift per front anchor pair: about a kip and a quarter, call it six-fifty per anchor, tension, cycling, in a room whose slab may be a topping over structure. And now the ganging pays: eight cabinets bolted into one row resist cross-aisle overturning as individuals, the row doesn't help that direction, but along-aisle they act as one long unit, trivially stable. Cross-aisle rules the design.

Step three, the anchors. Half-inch post-installed screw anchors or wedge anchors, seismic-qualified per their evaluation report, three-and-three-quarter-inch embedment in the slab. Cracked-concrete tension breakout per anchor: on the order of three to four kips design; against six-fifty pounds, comfortable, until the commandment: overstrength, times two: thirteen hundred, still fine. Shear: three hundred per anchor, easy. Edge distances: cabinets against a wall put rear anchors near the slab edge at a trench, verify, and cabinet manufacturers' base channels set gauge and bolt sizes, the interface again. Special inspection: required, documented.

Step four, the overlays that make this cell of the matrix unique. The fire standard: spacing between cabinet groups, ventilation and off-gas detection coordination, and it explicitly requires seismic anchorage per the building code, your calc is a code-compliance document for the fire marshal too. The room: floor loading two-fifty-plus psf that we flagged in the floors lesson, and containment curbs. And operational reality: cabinets get replaced, anchors get reused or reinstalled, spec the re-anchoring rule now.
