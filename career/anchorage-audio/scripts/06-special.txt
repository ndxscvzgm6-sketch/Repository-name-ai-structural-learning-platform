Lecture six. Special situations: where real projects live. The textbook case is a rigid box bolted to a thick slab. Data centers are almost never the textbook case. Six situations, each with its trap and its fix.

Situation one: vibration isolators, and this is the big one. Generators, chillers, pumps, and fans sit on springs so their vibration doesn't enter the structure. But springs are an earthquake's toy: the equipment can bounce, slam, and amplify. The code's response is severe: isolated equipment takes the highest amplification factor, and, where there's a gap in the load path, an air gap or soft-stopped clearance beyond nominal, the design force on the anchorage effectively doubles. The hardware answer is seismic snubbers: bumpers that let the machine float in service but catch it in an earthquake, or spring mounts with built-in seismic housings. The review question: are the isolators seismically rated, are snubbers shown, and did the calc use the isolated-equipment factors, a vendor cut sheet that says vibration isolation without the word seismic is a finding.

Situation two: the housekeeping pad, the four-inch concrete curb every machine sits on. The classic catastrophic oversight: anchors designed beautifully into the pad, and the pad connected to the slab by nothing but a cold joint and hope. The whole assembly slides as a unit. Every pad needs designed dowels or roughened-interface shear friction to the structural slab, and pad edges need to be far enough from anchors for the breakout cones, undersized pads with two-inch edge distances are epidemic. Pad thickness, pad reinforcement, pad-to-slab connection: three numbers every pad detail must show.

Situation three: anchoring up high, to roof structure. Rooftop units and hung distribution attach to steel and metal deck, and the rules change: no concrete cones, but now member local effects, deck attachments that are never allowed to carry component tension in some systems, and coordination with the joist manufacturer, joists take concentrated loads only where designed for them. Hung piping, busway, and tray use trapeze supports with transverse and longitudinal bracing at code-prescribed spacings, and the displacement demand from lecture two shows up at every joint crossing.

Situation four: tanks and vessels. Day tanks, thermal storage, fuel. Sloshing lengthens periods and adds hydrodynamic force, flat-bottom tanks have their own design standards, and anchorage must respect an empty case that maximizes uplift and a full case that maximizes everything else.

Situation five: the lithium battery rack, our third worked calc. Tall, narrow, heavy, and governed by overturning, with a code overlay: the energy storage standard demands spacing, ventilation, and structural anchorage, and rack systems are usually vendor-engineered, storage-rack-style structures where your job is the interface: the floor reactions the vendor's calc delivers, and the slab and anchors that must receive them. Never accept vendor reactions without the load combinations and the applicable code edition stated.

Situation six: the insurer's overlay. Major owners carry factory mutual style insurance, whose data sheets impose their own anchorage and wind criteria, sometimes stricter than code. The basis-of-design must say which governs where. An owner's engineer knows to ask.

Takeaways: isolators double forces unless snubbed and rated. Pads need dowels and edge distance, always ask for three numbers. High attachments follow steel rules and displacement demands. Tanks slosh, racks overturn, and the insurer gets a vote. Now, the calculators come out: worked calc one, the generator.
