Lecture five. The anchoring chapter, part two: shear, interaction, and the ductility commandments. Tension was six failure modes; shear adds three, then the code asks the deepest question in anchorage: when the earthquake exceeds your numbers, and someday it will, what yields first?

Shear mode one: steel shear, the bolt shank cuts. Ductile-ish, predictable. Mode two: concrete breakout in shear, an anchor near an edge, loaded toward that edge, pops out a half-cone of concrete toward the free face. Capacity scales with edge distance raised to the one point five, and this mode tyrannizes real designs: equipment pads and slab edges put anchors close to edges constantly, and shear-toward-edge breakout is very often the governing number on the whole calc. Direction matters: the same anchor loaded parallel to the edge gets double the capacity of loaded toward it. Mode three: pryout, a short stocky anchor in thick concrete levers a chunk out backwards, checked as a multiple of the tension breakout.

Then interaction: real anchors carry tension and shear simultaneously, and the code combines them, the classic check allows a tri-linear or a five-thirds power interaction, full capacity in one direction only if the other is below twenty percent. Practical translation: an anchor at eighty percent tension has little shear budget left, and the worked calcs will show both.

Now the commandments, the seismic design philosophy that separates professionals from bolt-pickers. The code's fear is brittle failure: concrete breakout is sudden, total, and non-negotiable. So for seismic tension, you must satisfy one of four options, learn them as a menu. Option A, the aristocrat: make the anchor itself the ductile fuse, a deep-embedded rod whose steel yields and stretches before any concrete mode can occur, with the concrete capacities comfortably above the steel's expected strength, stretch length matters, hence sleeves or unbonded length. Option B: put the fuse in the attachment, a base plate that yields in bending, a designed ductile element, protecting the anchor behind it. Option C, the workhorse of real data center projects: overstrength, design the anchor for the amplified force, the chapter-thirteen demand multiplied by the system overstrength factor, omega zero, typically around two, no ductility claimed, just brute capacity headroom. Option D exists for lighter conditions: design for the maximum force the component can deliver. On review, your question is simply: which option does this calc claim, and does the arithmetic actually deliver it? A calc that quotes overstrength but forgot to multiply is the classic bust.

Three field realities to carry. First, supplementary reinforcement: hairpins and ties placed to catch the breakout cone don't just add capacity, they change the failure story, and good pad details always include them. Second, installation is half the strength: adhesive anchors demand cleaned holes, correct cure, and, for sustained tension, special inspection; expansion anchors demand torque verification. The code's answer is mandated special inspection, and an owner's engineer verifies the inspection reports exist, paper protects concrete. Third, welding to embeds: when equipment is welded to cast-in plates instead of bolted, the plate's anchors follow all the same rules, and the weld itself becomes a link in the load path, size it, don't assume it.

Takeaways: shear adds steel, edge breakout, and pryout, and edges tyrannize. Tension plus shear interact, budget accordingly. The four seismic options, ductile anchor, ductile attachment, overstrength, or delivered-force, and overstrength is the daily driver. And installation quality, inspections, and reports are part of the design. Next: the special situations that fill actual data center projects.
