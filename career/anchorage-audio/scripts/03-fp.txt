Lecture three. The force equation. This is the heart of chapter thirteen: how big is the earthquake force on a component? I'll teach the classic formulation first, because it's what most practitioners know and what the reference guides use, then the newer re-formulation, because the current edition restructured it and you should speak both dialects.

The classic form, from the twenty-sixteen edition and earlier, reads: F p equals zero point four, times a p, times S D S, times W p, times the height amplifier, one plus two z over h, all divided by the ratio R p over I p. Let's give every symbol its meaning, because each one encodes a physical idea. S D S is the site's short-period design acceleration, the basic ground shaking. W p is the component's operating weight, and hear that word, operating: a chiller full of water, a generator with its coolant and oil, a battery rack fully populated. The height amplifier, one plus two z over h, where z is the component's height in the building and h is the roof height, says: buildings amplify shaking as it goes up. At grade, the multiplier is one. On the roof, z equals h, and it's three. The same chiller feels three times the force on the roof as in the yard. That single factor drives entire cost decisions about where equipment lives.

Then the two personality factors. a p, the amplification factor, between one and two point five: is the component rigid, following the floor obediently, or flexible, resonating and amplifying? A squat transformer: one. A flexible tank, a rack on isolators: two point five. And R p, the response modification factor, one to three point five or so: how ductile is the component and its attachment, how much can it yield without failing? Ductile steel equipment: higher. Brittle attachments: lower. Dividing by R p over I p, with I p at one point five for us, means: less ductility, more force; more importance, more force. Finally, floor and ceiling: F p need not exceed one point six S D S I p W p, and can't be taken less than zero point three S D S I p W p.

Feel the equation with quick mental math at S D S of one point zero, importance one point five. A rigid component on grade: force around forty percent of its weight. The same component on the roof: over a hundred percent. A flexible, low-ductility component on the roof: pinned at the cap, one hundred sixty percent, times one point five, two point four times its own weight, sideways. When equipment people gasp at anchor sizes, that's why.

Now the newer dialect. The twenty-twenty-two edition rebuilt the same physics with sharper tools: the height amplifier became a factor called H f, calibrated to real building response including a term that grows steeply near the roof. The structure's own ductility now helps you, through a reduction factor R mu, stiffer, less ductile buildings transmit more. And the component personality factors were re-tabulated as C A R, the component resonance category, and R p o, the component strength factor. Same skeleton: ground shaking, times height effect, times component personality, times importance, times weight, with caps. If you learn the classic form's physics, the new tables are just recalibrated knobs. On any project: use the edition the project is under, and say so in the calc's basis section.

Two closing subtleties. Vertical earthquake rides along: plus or minus twenty percent of S D S times dead load, concurrent. And overturning is usually the real fight: F p acts at the component's center of gravity, typically well above the anchors, so the moment, force times height of center of gravity, divided by the anchor group's lever arm, minus the stabilizing weight, gives the anchor tension. That subtraction is where operating weight versus empty weight matters, empty governs uplift. You'll see all of it in the worked calcs. Next: how anchors themselves fail.
