Lecture four. The concrete anchoring chapter, part one: tension. Chapter thirteen gives you the demand. The concrete code's anchoring chapter, chapter seventeen, decides whether your anchor survives it. Its genius is brutal honesty: an anchor in tension can fail six different ways, and you must check every one, because the concrete usually fails before the steel.

Picture a single anchor bolt in a slab, pulled straight up. Failure mode one: steel rupture. The bolt itself breaks. Clean, predictable, and, importantly, the only truly ductile mode. Mode two: concrete breakout, the star of the show. The anchor rips out a cone of concrete, radiating at about thirty-five degrees from the embedded end. Breakout strength scales with the embedment depth raised to the one point five power, so depth is your best friend, but the cone needs room: an anchor near an edge, or near its neighbors, can't develop a full cone, and the code handles this through projected areas, you compute the idealized cone area, then clip it for every edge and every overlapping neighbor, and the capacity drops proportionally. Groups of anchors close together are one big shared cone, not four independent ones, the single most common capacity error in amateur calcs. Mode three: pullout, the anchor head or expansion mechanism pulls through the concrete locally, a per-anchor value from testing. Mode four: side-face blowout, deep anchor near an edge bursts the side cover. Mode five, for adhesive anchors: bond failure, the glue-to-concrete interface lets go at some stress over the bonded surface, and adhesives bring their own dramas: sensitivity to hole cleaning, temperature, sustained load creep, there's a separate check for anchors under permanent tension. Mode six: splitting during installation, controlled by spacing and edge minimums.

Overlay three code-wide rules. Rule one: assume cracked concrete unless you can prove otherwise. Slabs crack, earthquake regions especially, and cracked capacities run thirty to forty percent below uncracked. Every table value you pull, ask: cracked or uncracked? Rule two: strength reduction factors are stingier than beam design, and they reward reinforcement: supplementary reinforcement, rebar positioned to arrest the breakout cone, buys you a better phi factor. Rule three: in seismic applications, tension capacities generally take a further reduction, and, bigger than any single factor, the seismic ductility requirement, which is lecture five's centerpiece.

One practical taxonomy before we close, because the hardware matters. Cast-in anchors: headed bolts and rods placed before the pour, strongest, cheapest, but they demand coordination, exactly what a reviewed embed drawing is for. Post-installed mechanical anchors: expansion and screw anchors, and undercut anchors, the aristocrats, which carve their own bearing head downhole. Post-installed adhesive anchors: threaded rod in structural epoxy. And the governing rule for all post-installed hardware: it must carry a current qualification report, evaluation service reports in the U S, proving it was tested to the anchoring standard for cracked concrete and seismic use. On any review: no report, or a report without the seismic category, and the anchor is fiction. Ask for the report number in the calc.

Takeaways: six tension failure modes, and the concrete modes usually govern. Breakout is a thirty-five degree cone, clipped by edges and shared by groups. Cracked concrete is the default reality. And post-installed anchors live and die by their test reports. Next: shear, interaction, and the ductility commandments.
