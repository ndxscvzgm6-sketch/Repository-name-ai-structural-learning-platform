Lesson six. The A I revolution: why every assumption changed. This is the shortest story in the course, and the most consequential for your career. For twenty years, a server rack drew five to ten kilowatts, weighed a few hundred pounds loaded, and was cooled by air. Every data center on Earth was designed around those numbers. Then A I training arrived.

A training cluster isn't many independent servers; it's tens of thousands of G P U chips working as one machine, and they need to be packed as close together as physics allows, because the speed of light is a real constraint on how fast they talk. Density is destiny. So the flagship A I rack today, N-VIDIA's G B two hundred class, draws about a hundred twenty to a hundred thirty kilowatts, ten times the old assumption, and the deployed compute rack weighs around three thousand pounds, roughly a ton and a half, in a two-foot-by-four-foot footprint. And the roadmap keeps climbing: the next generations are heading toward four thousand pounds and several hundred kilowatts per rack. One careful note for your calcs: the three-thousand-pound figure is the deployed compute rack; the full delivered system, with its switch rack and cooling unit, ships as several pieces totaling far more, always confirm the exact configuration.

Now cascade the consequences, they all land near your desk. First, floors. Old data hall design assumed maybe a hundred and fifty to two hundred fifty pounds per square foot. A I halls are being designed at three hundred to five hundred, with rack point loads that overwhelm traditional raised access floors, which is why new A I halls increasingly put racks straight on structural slab, and why floor flatness specs suddenly matter, you're rolling ton-and-a-half cabinets on casters and they must sit dead level. Second, liquid: a hundred twenty kilowatts cannot be air-cooled, so the cold plates, coolant manifolds, C D Us, and leak containment from last lesson become standard equipment, all of it weight, all of it yours. Third, everything upstream multiplies: ten times the power per rack means more transformers, more U P S, more batteries, more generators, more busway, more grey space, more building. And fourth, the retrofit wave: thousands of existing buildings designed for the old world are being assessed for the new loads, and evaluating an existing structure for triple the load is forensic, existing-buildings engineering, which, if you've done restoration and assessment work, is exactly your background walking through the door.

The scale of the moment, for context: hyperscalers are spending several hundred billion dollars a year on this buildout, gigawatt campuses are being announced routinely, and the binding constraints are power, cooling, and people who understand mission-critical facilities. The structural people who can speak these numbers fluently are scarce.

So here's the sentence to be able to say in an interview, calmly: I understand that a G B two-hundred-class rack at roughly a hundred twenty kilowatts and three thousand pounds forces liquid cooling, drives slab point loads and flatness requirements, multiplies the upstream electrical plant, and turns legacy halls into structural assessment projects. One sentence, and you've separated yourself from every generic structural candidate in the room.

Takeaways: ten times the power, triple-plus the weight, same footprint. Slab on grade beats raised floor at A I density. Everything upstream scales with the racks. And the retrofit wave is a forensic engineer's home game. Next: your gravity systems lesson.
