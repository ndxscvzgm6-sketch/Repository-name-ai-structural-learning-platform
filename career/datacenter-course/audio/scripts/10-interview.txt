Lesson ten. Speaking the language: interview preparation. Everything you've learned only pays when you can say it out loud, under pressure, in front of people who do this daily. So this lesson is a rehearsal. I'll pose the questions an owner-side structural interview actually asks, give you a beat to think, then model a strong answer's skeleton. Practice these on walks until they're reflexes.

Question one: walk me through the loads on a data center floor. Strong answer: start with the truth that the code minimum is a floor, not a target, one hundred psf versus real racks at four hundred plus on their footprint; name modern A I design values, three hundred to five hundred; call out battery rooms at two-fifty to four-fifty and five-ton P D Us; and land on method: I design to the equipment schedule and vendor point loads, and I check the caster roll-in case, because concentrated loads govern more often than uniform ones.

Question two: what's different about a data center roof? Skeleton: it's a mechanical yard, chillers and towers at operating, water-filled weight, vibration isolation, screen walls as wind sails with snow drifting behind, ten to fifty psf of hung busway and piping below, and relentless coordination because equipment moves.

Question three: how does A I change structural design? The one-sentence version you already own: ten times the rack power, a ton and a half per cabinet, forced liquid cooling with its manifolds and containment, multiplied upstream electrical plant, slab-on-grade instead of raised floor, and a retrofit wave that's really structural assessment of existing buildings.

Question four: explain concurrent maintainability and what it means for you. Skeleton: any single component can be serviced without dropping load, that's the tier-three idea, and structurally it means removal paths, hoist points, and openings sized so a chiller can leave the building while everything runs. Reliability lives in corridors and door schedules, not just one-lines.

Question five: what risk category is a data center? The nuanced answer from lesson eight, owners elect three or four for continuity, importance factor one point five, and I confirm the basis of design first. Follow with the anchorage empire: chapter thirteen, thousands of components, stamped calcs above four hundred pounds.

Question six: you're reviewing a consultant's structural package, what do you check first? This is the owner's-engineer audition. Skeleton: basis of design first, criteria, risk category, equipment loads assumed. Then floor loads against the actual rack plan. Then the anchorage scope, is every component over threshold covered? Then maintainability paths. Then deviations from the owner's prototype. Code compliance is necessary; conformance to the standard is the job.

And the behavioral layer, because roughly half of these interviews is stories: prepare five, in the situation-action-result shape. A time you caught an error in someone else's work, forensics gold. A coordination fight you resolved with mechanical or electrical. A schedule crunch where you protected quality. A field problem you dispositioned on your feet. And a time you learned a domain fast, this course is literally that story.

Close with your positioning sentence, the one from the career platform: I've designed high-rises at LeMessurier and judged existing structures in forensics, I speak enough of the data center machine to anticipate its loads, and my discipline is where the reliability promises physically live.
