Lesson two. The anatomy of a data center. Every discipline has its map words. Learn these and design meetings snap into focus.

The first cut is the most important: whitespace versus grey space. Whitespace is the room the money lives in: raised or slab floor, tightly cooled, badge-controlled, holding the rows of server racks. Everything else is grey space: electrical rooms, battery rooms, the mechanical plant, generator yards, loading docks, the back of house that keeps the whitespace alive. And here's a trend worth quoting: as racks get denser, the grey space grows relative to the white, the machine supporting each square foot of computing keeps getting bigger.

Zoom into the whitespace. The atomic unit is the rack: a standardized steel cabinet, about two feet wide, roughly four feet deep, six to seven feet tall. Racks line up in rows, rows face each other in pairs, and the pairing is thermal: cold aisles where the intakes face each other, hot aisles where the exhausts do. Rows fill a data hall, a hall might hold hundreds of racks, and a building holds several halls. For you, right away: a row of racks is a line of heavy, concentrated, rolling loads on your floor, and the trend line points straight up.

Now zoom out to the campus. A modern hyperscale building runs to hundreds of thousands of square feet, drawing tens to hundreds of megawatts. Campuses cluster several buildings around their own electrical substation, plus generator yards, fuel storage, cooling plant, and water infrastructure. The building you design is one organ in a larger creature.

Three business models, because they shape your structural brief. Hyperscale: one owner, Google, Meta, Amazon, building standardized prototypes at maximum speed, often for A I. Colocation, colo for short: an operator like Equinix or Q T S renting space and power to many tenants, more flexibility, often multi-story in cities. And edge: small facilities near users for low latency, often prefabricated modules. Hyperscale means repetition, a kit of parts you'll reuse dozens of times, so an hour saved on the typical bay pays back a thousandfold across the fleet. That fleet thinking is the deepest cultural difference from one-off building design.

The structural angle to close: read the program before the loads. Whether a project is hyperscale, colo, or edge tells you the structural story before you see a single number: repetition and schedule for hyperscale, flexibility and multi-tenant loading for colo, modular connections for edge. Owners think in fleets, not buildings.

Takeaways: whitespace earns, grey space sustains, and grey is growing. Rack, row, hall, building, campus, that's the scale ladder. And the business model is the structural brief in disguise. Next: the power train, from the utility grid to the chip.
