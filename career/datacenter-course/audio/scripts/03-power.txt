Lesson three. The power train, utility to chip. Power is the master constraint of this industry: sites are chosen for it, buildings are sized in megawatts, and the phrase speed to power decides billion-dollar deals. Walk the electron with me once, and eighty percent of the grey space will make sense forever.

Power arrives from the utility at high voltage and lands at the campus substation, where transformers the size of small houses step it down to medium voltage, and they're among the heaviest objects on the site, sitting on massive pads with oil containment. Medium voltage runs to switchgear, rooms of circuit breakers that route and protect the flow. Unit transformers then step down to four hundred eighty volts for use, roughly one transformer for every two and a half to three megawatts.

Then comes the most important machine you'll rarely see: the U P S, the uninterruptible power supply. The grid blinks, storms, sags. Computers tolerate none of it. The U P S, typically rooms of batteries feeding electronics that condition the power continuously, carries the entire critical load for the seconds-to-minutes gap between a utility failure and backup power. Batteries mean battery rooms, and file this now: a battery room is one of the heaviest floor loads in the building, lead-acid installations run two hundred fifty to four hundred fifty pounds per square foot, and the newer lithium systems cut that dramatically but bring their own fire code. If someone hands you just an electrical room, ask what's in it.

Behind the U P S: distribution. P D Us, power distribution units, five-to-ten-thousand-pound floor cabinets, split power to the rows. Overhead busway, rigid power rail hung from your roof structure with seismic bracing, drops a connection to each rack. And every rack gets two cords, A and B, from two separate paths, redundancy made visible.

And for when the grid truly dies: generators. Diesel units around two to two and a half megawatts each, deployed in ranks, one famous Amazon campus in Virginia runs ninety-three of them. Each engine has a day tank of fuel nearby, refilled from bulk tanks holding a campus target of twenty-four to forty-eight hours at full load. Generators start in about ten seconds, synchronize to each other, and pick up the building in stages, all automatic, all rehearsed. Structurally, a generator yard is a project in itself: twenty-to-thirty-five-ton machines that shake, on mass-rule pads, with exhaust stacks catching wind, fuel tanks weighing hundreds of thousands of pounds, containment berms, and anchorage designed to the highest seismic category, because backup power must survive the very event it exists for.

The A I kicker: when rack power jumps tenfold, everything upstream multiplies with it, more transformers, more U P S modules, more batteries, more busway, more weight, more rooms, more of your building.

Takeaways: utility, substation, switchgear, transformers, U P S, P D U, busway, dual cords, that's the train. The U P S buys minutes; generators buy days; batteries crush floors; busway hangs from your steel. And every kilowatt of compute drags a kilogram tax through your structure.
