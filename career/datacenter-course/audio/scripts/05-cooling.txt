Lesson five. Cooling and thermal design. One law governs half the building: every watt of electricity that goes into a server comes out as heat, all of it, continuously. A hundred-megawatt data center is a hundred-megawatt heater that must never warm up. That's why the cooling plant rivals the power plant in size, cost, and, for you, structural tonnage.

The classic approach is air. Room-scale units push cold air to the server intakes and swallow the hot exhaust. Two acronyms, one letter apart, and the letter matters: a CRAC has a Compressor, it's a self-contained air conditioner; a CRAH is just a coil and fan, Handling air over chilled water made elsewhere. Big facilities use CRAHs, because the real cooling happens in a central chilled-water plant: chillers making cold water, pumps circulating thousands of gallons a minute, and cooling towers on the roof or in the yard rejecting the heat to the sky, mostly by evaporating water. Airflow discipline does the rest: hot aisles and cold aisles, physically separated with containment so the streams never mix, and economizers, free cooling, that shut the refrigeration off whenever outside air is cool enough, which is why data centers love cool, dry climates.

Two scoreboard numbers to own. P U E, power usage effectiveness: total building power divided by computing power, perfect is one point zero, the industry average has sat near one point five four for years, and the best fleets, Google, Meta, run about one point zero nine. And W U E, water usage effectiveness, because evaporative cooling drinks: a big campus can evaporate hundreds of thousands of gallons a day, which is now a genuine political issue. Here's the trade to remember: evaporation saves energy but spends water; dry cooling saves water but spends energy; you can't win both, and the industry's current answer is closed-loop designs that fill the pipes once and reject heat dry.

Then A I arrived and broke air cooling entirely. Air tops out around forty to fifty kilowatts per rack; A I racks draw a hundred twenty plus. So liquid now goes to the chip: cold plates clamped onto the processors, coolant loops through every rack, and a new device, the C D U, coolant distribution unit, exchanging heat between the clean rack loop and the facility water. Immersion, dunking whole servers in tanks of fluid, exists at the exotic edge, and those tanks are extraordinarily heavy.

Now the structural angle, and it's massive. The rooftop or yard carries the rejection plant: chillers and towers in ranks, always designed at operating weight, full of water, not shipping weight, on vibration isolation, wind on the screens hiding them, snow drifting behind. Thousands of tons of mechanical equipment can live on a hyperscale roof. The piping is heavy: a two-foot water main carries about two hundred pounds per foot before the steel. And liquid cooling brings weight into the whitespace itself: filled manifolds down every row, C D U cabinets, leak containment curbs, and floor drains in rooms that never had them.

Takeaways: every watt in is a watt of heat out. CRAC has the compressor, CRAH just the coil. P U E one point five four average versus one point zero nine elite, and the water-energy trade has no free lunch. Air dies at fifty kilowatts, liquid reaches the chip. And the heat's exit route, roof plant, piping, C D Us, is paved with structural load. Next: the A I revolution in numbers.
