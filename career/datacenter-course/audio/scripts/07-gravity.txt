Lesson seven. Structural design part one: gravity and the building shell. Now we're fully in your lane. The theme of everything that follows: a data center is a building shaped entirely by the equipment it carries, on a schedule that dominates every decision.

The shell first. Most hyperscale data centers are single-story or low-rise, long-span boxes with wide bays keeping the halls column-free. The dominant recipe: tilt-up concrete wall panels, cast flat on the floor slab and craned upright, with a long-span steel roof, joists and joist girders or trusses. Why? Speed and repeatability. The owner is building the same box dozens of times, and every week of schedule is enormous revenue. Precast panels and pre-engineered steel play the same game; cast-in-place shows up in multi-story urban colos. If you want the full tilt-up discipline, panel design, lifting, bracing, there's an entire sister course on it; here, just own the logic: schedule is the design driver, and standardized details beat clever ones.

Now floors, the heart of your job, and here's the number-one fact: the code minimum is dangerously below reality. The code book value for computer floors is one hundred pounds per square foot, or a two-thousand-pound point load. But a single loaded A I rack puts four-hundred-plus pounds per square foot on its own footprint. So nobody designs to the minimum: military standards say one-fifty, legacy enterprise practice said two-fifty to three-fifty, and modern A I halls run three hundred to five hundred, engineered to the actual rack layout. The real method is always: get the equipment schedule, get the vendor point loads, design to those.

Two floor subtleties that mark expertise. First, the raised access floor: removable panels on little pedestals, historically creating a cold-air plenum below. It is not the structural floor, the loads pass through the pedestals to the slab, and the panels themselves are rated for maybe a thousand kilograms per square meter, far below A I rack loads, which is why dense halls are abandoning raised floors for slab on grade. Know which world you're in. Second, the sneaky governing case: concentrated loads. A ton-and-a-half rack rolls in on four to six casters, five to seven hundred pounds per caster on a few square inches, and that roll-in case often governs the slab, along with floor flatness specs so the racks sit level.

Beyond the racks: battery rooms at two-fifty to four-fifty pounds per square foot. P D U cabinets at five to ten thousand pounds. Housekeeping pads under everything. And then look up: the roof is a second equipment yard. Chillers, cooling towers, air handlers, screen walls, sometimes solar, thousands of tons of mechanical equipment at operating, water-filled weight, plus hung loads below: busway, cable tray, and piping at ten to fifty pounds per square foot where a warehouse carries five. Design the roof for the machine it carries, and coordinate every unit move, because when mechanical relocates a chiller, your framing changes.

Takeaways: tilt-up plus steel wins on schedule and repetition. Ignore the hundred-pound code floor, design to the equipment, three to five hundred in A I halls. Raised floor is not structure, and casters govern more than people think. Batteries and P D Us crush floors; the roof is a mechanical yard at operating weight.
