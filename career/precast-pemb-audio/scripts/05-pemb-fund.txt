Lecture five. P E M B fundamentals: the system, and who designs what. A pre-engineered metal building is not a steel building you happened to buy; it's a different contracting universe with its own structural logic. Understand the universe and the engineering follows.

The structural system first. The signature is the tapered rigid frame: columns and rafters built up from welded plate, deep where the moments are big, at the knees, shallow where they're small, at pins and ridge. Frames march down the building at twenty to thirty foot bays, spanning clear widths from sixty to two hundred plus feet. Between frames: cold-formed Z and C purlins on the roof, girts on the walls, light, efficient, and braced against buckling by the cladding and by little flange braces you'll learn to look for. Longitudinally, the building resists wind and quake through rod or cable X-bracing in designated bays, or portal frames where doors interrupt. Everything is optimized to the pound: the manufacturer's software shaves plate thickness inch by inch, which is why P E M B is unbeatable on cost and speed, and why it has zero accidental reserve. There is no fat in a metal building. Every load you didn't tell the manufacturer about is a real problem, hold that thought for the next lecture.

Now the universe: who designs what. You, or the project's engineer of record, issue a performance specification and the loads: codes, wind, snow, seismic, and, critically, the collateral loads, everything you plan to hang. The manufacturer's engineer, licensed, stamping, designs the entire steel building: frames, purlins, girts, bracing, and issues the erection drawings and, the document that rules your life, the frame reactions: column-by-column forces the building delivers to its supports. Then you design the foundations for those reactions. See the trap in the sequence? Foundations are needed early; final reactions come late. Projects run on preliminary reactions, and the reviewer's discipline is version control: which reaction letter is the foundation design based on, and is it the final one? A foundation poured to superseded reactions is a genuinely common P E M B failure story.

And the reactions themselves have a personality: rigid frames push outward. Gravity on a gabled rigid frame generates horizontal thrust at the column bases, tens of kips outward on big spans. Foundations must resist it: hairpin bars lapping into the slab, tie rods across the building, or moment-resisting footings. The hairpin detail, cheap bars hooking the column anchor bolts into the slab-on-grade, is elegant and beloved, and it silently assumes the slab exists, is continuous, and never gets trenched. A data center yard building whose slab gets cut for conduit five years later just lost its foundation system. Reviewers write that assumption down.

Complete the anatomy: anchor bolts set by the foundation contractor to the manufacturer's setting plan, another survey-critical interface; standing-seam or screw-down roof sheeting, which doubles as purlin bracing, so re-roofing is a structural event; wall panels and their girts; and the brand-name accessories, canopies, mezzanines, crane runways, each an engineered add-on with its own loads.

Takeaways: tapered frames, light purlins, X-braced length, everything optimized to zero reserve. The manufacturer designs the building; you design the spec, the loads, and the foundations, to the right revision of the reactions. Frames thrust outward; hairpins and ties resist, with assumptions worth auditing. Next: the reviewer's gotcha list, starring the collateral-load trap that eats data centers.
