Lecture three. Precast connections and the volume-change obsession. Cast-in-place concrete is a monolith; precast is a kit of large parts. That means the structure literally is its connections, and precast connection design has one extra master that steel and cast-in-place barely think about: the concrete keeps moving.

Start with the movement. Concrete shrinks as it dries, for years. Prestressed concrete also creeps, shortening under its permanent compression, for years. And everything breathes daily and seasonally with temperature. Add it up: a hundred-foot precast wall run can demand a half-inch or more of accumulated movement capacity. Restrain it, weld every panel rigidly to its neighbors and the foundation, and the panels crack, connections tear, bearing seats spall. The industry's design philosophy is therefore: hold what must be held, release what must move. Every connection is designed with a job description: this one resists, that one slides, and the drawings say which is which. When you review a precast package, the first thing you trace is the movement plan: where does each panel run expand toward, which connections are slotted, and did anyone accidentally weld a hard point in the middle of a run.

Now the connection taxonomy, five families to name fluently. Bearing connections: gravity lands through bearing pads, elastomeric or plastic shims that spread load and permit rotation and slip; the pad is a designed element, hunt for its size and material in the calc. Tie-back or lateral connections: keep panels plumb and deliver wind and seismic, often slotted vertically so gravity stays on the bearing, weld-plate-to-embed with designed play. Alignment connections: panel-to-panel edges, controlling relative bow. Moment or fixed connections: rare, deliberate, expensive. And diaphragm connections: chord and shear plates stitching roof and floor members into a diaphragm, in seismic country these follow the same intermediate-precast rules you know from tilt-up: connections yield ductilely at one and a half times yield, or are capacity-protected. Same religion, plant-cast congregation.

Seismic adds its overlay: emulation, either make precast joints behave like cast-in-place through welded and grouted splices, or use the tested jointed systems with their own qualification. For your building types, the practical translation: the lateral system's precast connections carry explicit ductility or overstrength justification in the calc, or the calc is incomplete.

Then the erection reality. Precast erects fast: a panel every twenty minutes, but the structure is incomplete for weeks, exactly like your tilt-up bracing story: temporary bracing engineered by the erector, brace removal gated on completed connections, and, an extra precast wrinkle, grout. Column base grout, joint grout, and grouted splice sleeves have cure and verification requirements before loads land; premature loading of ungrouted joints is a recurring near-miss. Reviewers ask for the erection sequence drawings and the grout hold points.

Close with the interface list you'll audit forever: embeds cast into foundations by the sitework contractor to receive the precaster's connections, the single most error-prone handoff in the system, survey them; loads the precaster assumed versus loads the final mechanical plans deliver, hello, roof collateral; and tolerances at the trades: window and louver frames, equipment rails, all detailed to precast tolerance, not steel tolerance.

Takeaways: concrete moves for years, so connections hold or release by explicit design. Five families: bearing, tie-back, alignment, moment, diaphragm. Seismic wants emulation or tested ductility. Erection lives on temporary bracing and grout gates. Next: the review exercise, catch the flaws in a real-shaped package.
