Lecture two. Precast fundamentals: the factory, prestressing, and the panel family. Precast is concrete with a supply chain: cast in a plant, cured overnight, trucked to site, and erected like steel. That industrialization changes the engineering in four deep ways.

Way one: prestressing. Most structural precast is prestressed: high-strength steel strands are tensioned across a long casting bed, concrete is cast around them, and when it's strong enough, the strands are cut, compressing the concrete permanently. The member now fights cracking with a built-in squeeze: tension from loads must first overcome the stored compression. Results: longer spans, thinner sections, crack control, and camber, the members bow upward slightly, which the erector and the flooring people must expect. For panels, prestressing mostly serves handling and slenderness; for the double-tees and girders spanning your electrical rooms, it's the whole game. What you audit: the strand pattern and release strength assumptions, and the camber predictions against tolerance stacks.

Way two: the section zoo. Learn five names. Solid wall panels: your tilt-up analog, plant-made. Insulated sandwich panels: two concrete wythes over foam, the data center favorite, a full lecture ahead. Hollow-core slabs: extruded planks with voids, floors and roofs of support buildings. Double-tees: the long-span workhorses of parking and industrial roofs. And columns, beams, and spandrels completing total-precast frames. Every one arrives with plant drawings, stamped by the precaster's specialty engineer, that's the delegation model from lecture one.

Way three: handling governs, again. Just like tilt-up, the worst day in a precast member's life is early: stripping from the form at maybe three-thousand-p-s-i concrete, yard handling, trucking, ten-foot-wide loads on the interstate, and erection. Lifting devices, strongbacks, and the stripping-strength spec are the precaster's job, but reviewers verify the erection engineering exists, especially for tall wall panels and for members that brace nothing until connected. And trucking sets the module: panels beyond about twelve feet wide or seventy feet long get expensive fast, that's why precast buildings have the joints they have.

Way four: quality and tolerance culture. Plants run certified quality programs under the industry institute's plant certification, tighter tolerances than site work, real cover control, steam curing. But tolerance is a system: plant tolerance, plus erection tolerance, plus the interface tolerance to the trades. The handbook's tolerance chapter exists because the classic precast failure is not strength, it's a half-inch of accumulated misfit meeting a rigid connection detail with no adjustment left. Every good precast connection has designed adjustability: slotted inserts, shims, field-welded plates with tolerance built in.

And the sandwich panel deserves its own minute, since data centers love it. Two wythes: a structural wythe, typically the inner, and a facade wythe, over rigid insulation, tied together with connectors, the modern ones fiber-composite to kill thermal bridging. The critical design word: composite action. Fully composite panels act as one thick section, stiff and strong but thermally bridged and bowing-prone; non-composite panels act as two independent thin sections; real panels are partially composite, some percentage, defined by the tie system and testing. Everything follows from that percentage: strength, deflection, thermal bowing, even the lifting design. Which is why the single sharpest question you can ask a precaster is: what percent composite did you assume, and what test data supports it? Hold onto that; the review exercise will use it.
