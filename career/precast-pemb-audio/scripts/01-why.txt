Welcome to Precast and Metal Building Literacy, the companion to your tilt-up course. Seven lectures covering the two other industrial construction systems that dominate the data center world: plant-cast precast concrete, and pre-engineered metal buildings, P E M B for short. The hiring evidence says this pairing matters: the senior job postings at the hyperscalers and their design firms ask, verbatim, for tilt-up and precast concrete structures, and the owner-side listings name pre-engineered metal buildings as a preferred qualification. This series gets you to review-fluency on both: not to design a prestressed girder tomorrow, but to walk into a package review knowing exactly where these systems hide their bodies.

Why do these two systems matter to data centers specifically? Map the campus. The main data halls: tilt-up or precast shells, you know tilt-up cold now. Precast steps in when: the schedule wants panels cast in a factory while the site's still mud, when winter shuts down site casting, when panels need factory finishes or insulated sandwich construction at tight quality, and when repetition across a fleet rewards industrialized production. Whole hyperscale programs have switched to total-precast structures for exactly these reasons. Then look at the support buildings: gatehouses, warehouses, water treatment buildings, pump houses, sometimes generator enclosures and utility support structures: that's P E M B country, fast, cheap, clear-span steel buildings ordered like equipment from manufacturers, and increasingly, P E M B-style systems are bidding on the halls themselves because nothing beats their speed.

Here's the mental model that organizes both systems, and it's the same model: these are delegated-design worlds. In conventional design, you draw every member. In precast and P E M B, a specialist, the precaster's engineer, the metal building manufacturer's engineer, designs the components, and the project engineer, or the owner's engineer, you, designs the interfaces and audits the package. Your deliverables become: the performance specification, the loads you hand them, the foundations that receive their reactions, and the review that catches their assumptions colliding with your building's reality. Both industries run on this split, both have excellent specialist engineers, and both generate the same class of failures: not bad components, but orphaned interfaces, loads nobody handed over, drifts nobody checked compatibility against, foundations designed to reactions from a superseded frame run.

So the plan. Lectures two through four: precast, fundamentals and prestressing, then connections and the volume-change obsession, then a full narrated design-review exercise on a sandwich-panel package with planted flaws for you to catch. Lectures five through seven: P E M B, how the system works and who designs what, then the reviewer's gotcha list, especially the collateral-load trap that bites data centers, then the closing design-review exercise and how to sell this literacy in interviews.

One calibration before we start: the depth target. The people getting hired into the seats you want can, in one meeting, ask a precaster: what's your assumed percent composite on these sandwich panels, and what bearing pad movement did you design for? And ask a metal building supplier: what collateral load did you carry, and are your frame reactions final? Four questions, and the room knows they've done this. By lecture seven, those questions, and a dozen like them, will be yours. Let's start with how factory concrete actually works.
