Lesson sixteen. Operating the critical facility. Design gets a facility to day one. The next seven thousand days belong to operations, and the outage data from lesson three says operations is where availability is actually won or lost. Remember the number: about eighty-five percent of human-error outages are procedure failures.

So the discipline runs on a trinity of documents. S O Ps, standard operating procedures: how routine things are always done, shift rounds, valve lineups, escalations. M O Ps, methods of procedure: a step-by-step script for one specific planned activity, annual service on U P S two A, with prerequisites, back-out points, verification steps, and signatures. In a serious facility, nothing planned happens without an approved M O P, nothing. And E O Ps, emergency operating procedures: pre-decided responses to failures, loss of chilled water pressure in hall three, drilled until they're reflexes, because three in the morning is not when you want creativity. Wrap those in change management, formal review before anything touches a live system, exactly the gate that failed in Singapore, and regular drills, and you have the human redundancy layer.

Maintenance in a facility that can never stop is its own art. The concurrent maintainability we designed for exists precisely so you can service things while running. The strategy stack: preventive maintenance on the calendar, monthly generator runs under load, quarterly battery checks, annual infrared scans of every electrical connection, loose lugs glow before they fail. Predictive maintenance on top: vibration signatures, oil analysis, battery impedance trending, fix things because the data says they're drifting, before they announce themselves. And reliability-centered prioritization: rank assets by consequence and spend your attention accordingly. All of it shadowed by the lesson of the O V H fire: every open-panel hour is elevated risk. Maintenance windows get planned like small surgeries.

Then there's the failure mode nobody sees coming because it arrives as success: capacity creep. The facility fills up. I T load grows quarter by quarter, until one day the N-plus-one chiller plant is effectively N at peak, the U P S ride-through is shorter than the generator start window, and nobody ever decided any of that. The defense is capacity management: continuously tracking actual load against design margins, per hall, per bus, per cooling zone. An owner's engineer asks for the trend charts, not the nameplates.

One more piece of literacy: the Uptime Institute rates operations themselves, a certification called operational sustainability, covering staffing, training, and procedure discipline. It exists because a top-tier building run badly performs like a bottom-tier building. Design sets the ceiling; operations decides where under it you actually live.

And the structural engineer's part in a well-run facility is designed-in operability: equipment access and removal paths, repair time is physical, platforms and davits for safe rooftop work, clearances that make procedures performable, laydown space for the replacement chiller. A building that makes correct operation easy and wrong operation awkward is itself a reliability system, arguably the cheapest one on the project.

Takeaways: S O P, M O P, E O P, the procedure trinity, plus change control and drills. Maintain preventively, predictively, and by consequence. Watch capacity creep eat your margins silently.
