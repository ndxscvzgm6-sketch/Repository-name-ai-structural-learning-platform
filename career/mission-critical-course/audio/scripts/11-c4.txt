Lesson eleven. Liquid cooling systems engineering. Air cooling has a hard ceiling, around forty to fifty kilowatts per rack, even with perfect airflow discipline. Modern A I racks draw a hundred twenty to a hundred forty. So liquid now comes all the way to the chip, and a whole new plumbing architecture comes with it.

The architecture is two loops with a customs checkpoint between them. The inner loop, called the technology cooling system, touches the actual hardware: treated, filtered coolant flowing through cold plates clamped directly onto the processors. It must be immaculately clean, a clogged cold-plate microchannel is a dead G P U, and tightly controlled in temperature, flow, and pressure. The outer loop is just ordinary facility water headed to the chillers or dry coolers. And between them sits the C D U, the coolant distribution unit: a heat exchanger, variable-speed pumps, filtration, and controls, keeping the sacred inner loop isolated from the profane outer one. C D Us come as sidecars in the row or as big floor-mounted plant units, and their pumps are now on the critical-load list from last lesson: if C D U pumps stop, chips overheat in seconds.

Know the technology ladder. Rung one, dominant today: direct-to-chip cold plates, capturing seventy to ninety percent of the rack's heat in liquid, the rest still needs some air handling. Rung two: rear-door heat exchangers, a radiator door on the back of the rack, a good retrofit up to about eighty kilowatts. Rung three: immersion, dunking entire servers in tanks of dielectric fluid, the ultimate in heat capture, but the tanks are extraordinarily heavy and the exotic fluids face environmental regulatory pressure. Cold plates are winning the mainstream.

Here's the strategic insight that sounds paradoxical until it clicks: warm water is the prize. Chips don't need cold water; they need enough flow of warmish water, and the industry classifies facilities by supply temperature, the W classes, running up to forty-five degrees Celsius. If your hardware accepts water at forty or forty-five degrees, you can reject heat with dry coolers alone in most climates on Earth: no chillers, no evaporation, and the return water is warm enough to heat neighborhoods. Liquid cooling, done warm, can actually simplify the entire plant. That's why the W forty class matters more than it sounds.

But liquid brings new failure modes, and the reliability culture must extend to plumbing. Leaks over live electronics: leak-detection cable, drip trays, automatic isolation valves, and floor drainage in halls that never had drains. Coolant chemistry drift: corrosion or biology slowly fouling every cold plate in the fleet, a slow-motion common-mode failure, so chemistry gets monitored like battery cells. And service discipline: every server swap involves quick-disconnect fittings that must not drip.

For the structural engineer, liquid redistributes weight all over your floor plan: filled manifolds and piping down every row, hung or trenched, with seismic bracing. C D U skids as new concentrated loads. Containment curbs and slopes. And if immersion shows up, tank point loads that demand slab-on-grade. The floor that carried an air-cooled world was never asked these questions.

Takeaways: two loops, C D U between them. Cold plates dominate; immersion is the heavy exotic. Warm water, W forty and up, can eliminate chillers entirely. And liquid's failure modes, leaks, chemistry, disconnects, get the full mission-critical treatment. Next: fire.
