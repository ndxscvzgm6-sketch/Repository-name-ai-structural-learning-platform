Lesson two. The mathematics of availability. Reliability sounds like a vibe until someone opens a spreadsheet. Four simple ideas underlie every uptime claim, every tier rating, every redundancy debate. Master them and you can out-argue most people in the room.

Idea one: the nines. Availability is the fraction of time a service is up. Ninety-nine percent sounds impressive, until you realize it allows three and a half days of downtime a year. Add a nine: ninety-nine point nine percent still allows almost nine hours. Four nines: about fifty-three minutes a year. Five nines: five minutes. Each added nine costs roughly ten times more engineering and discipline. So when a salesman says five nines, you now hear: five minutes a year, prove it.

Idea two: component availability. Every machine has two numbers: how long it typically runs between failures, and how long it stays broken when it fails. Availability is simply uptime divided by total time. Take a good U P S: say it fails once every eleven years on average, and takes eight hours to repair. That's ninety-nine point nine nine two percent available. Sounds superb. Hold that thought.

Idea three: chains multiply down. Put components in series, where each one is needed, and you multiply their availabilities. Utility connection, times switchgear, times that superb U P S, times distribution, times the busway. Five excellent components, each around four nines, chain out to roughly ninety-nine point nine five percent, nearly four hours of expected downtime a year. From excellent parts! A chain is always worse than its weakest link, and it only gets worse as it grows. This is the mathematical proof that a single-path facility can never be truly high-availability, no matter how premium its equipment.

Idea four, the big one: parallel paths multiply the failure probabilities instead. Two independent paths, each ninety-nine point nine five, fail together only when both are down at once: multiply the tiny failure fractions, and you get about six nines and a half, theoretical seconds per year. Hours became seconds. Not by buying better components, by buying two of them and independence. Say it as a slogan: topology beats components. You cannot buy a perfect U P S; you can buy two ordinary ones and a promise they never fail together.

And that promise is the fine print that separates professionals from brochures. The parallel math assumes independent failures. Real facilities violate independence constantly: both paths pass through one room, and a fire takes both. One control system commands both, and a bad firmware update takes both. One utility, one operating crew, one procedure. These are called common-mode failures, and they're why real facilities never hit the theoretical numbers, why the highest tier requires physically separating redundant systems into different rooms, and why, when someone quotes you a string of nines, your first question is: independent how?

For the structural engineer, that compartmentalization is your work: fire-rated walls between the A-side and B-side electrical rooms, separated risers, sometimes separate buildings. And half of the repair-time number is physical too: a chiller that takes three days to remove because nobody left a removal path has tripled its repair time and dragged the whole facility's availability down with it.

Takeaways. Four nines is fifty-three minutes. Chains multiply down, parallel multiplies failure away. Topology beats components. And common-mode failures, shared rooms, shared controls, shared humans, are where the theory goes to die. Which sets up lesson three: what actually takes facilities down.
