Welcome to Mission-Critical Facility Engineering. Nineteen lessons that take you through the full discipline: power, cooling, reliability, fire, controls, commissioning, and operations. By the end, you'll be able to sit in a data center design review and follow every acronym, every argument, and every dollar.

Lesson one. What mission-critical actually means. Here's the definition that organizes everything: a mission-critical facility is one where the defining question is not "what does this building do," but "what happens in the first sixty seconds after it stops." Answer that honestly, and every design decision follows: the redundancy, the generators, the testing rituals, the money.

Walk the family with me. Data centers: when they stop, digital services die, banks freeze, and for an AI training run, weeks of compute can be lost; major operators lose a million dollars an hour or more. Hospitals: operating rooms and life support; codes literally mandate that generators pick up the load within ten seconds. Semiconductor fabs: a voltage sag lasting milliseconds scraps millions of dollars of wafers. Trading floors: market access and regulators. Control centers, air traffic, nine one one, the power grid: when they stop, the people who manage everyone else's emergencies go blind. Different buildings, one discipline. The same reliability logic, the same commissioning culture, the same vocabulary moves across all of them, and so do careers.

Now, hold this structure for the whole course: the discipline stands on three pillars. Pillar one, topology: the redundancy architecture on paper. What's duplicated, what's independent. Pillar two, physical infrastructure: the actual machines, generators, chillers, batteries, that implement the topology. Pillar three, operations: the humans, procedures, and maintenance that keep the promise for twenty years. A facility fails when any one pillar is weak. And here's the industry's uncomfortable secret, which we'll see in hard data in lesson three: the third pillar, the human one, fails most often.

Finally, the economics, because they justify everything that looks overbuilt. In twenty sixteen, a power failure in Delta Air Lines' data center grounded about two thousand flights and cost the airline roughly a hundred and fifty million dollars. One incident. British Airways, the next year: seventy-five thousand passengers stranded over a holiday weekend because power was restored incorrectly. Against numbers like that, a second generator plant and a permanent testing team are cheap insurance. That asymmetry, infrastructure cost is trivial next to failure cost, is the economic engine of this whole sector, and the reason its engineers out-earn ordinary building engineers.

And for you specifically, as a structural engineer: in this world, the building is a component of a machine whose availability is being purchased. Your risk category, your equipment anchorage, your removal paths, all trace back to a business decision about consequence. Learn to argue structure in the language of consequence, and you'll speak like an owner's engineer.

Takeaways. Mission-critical is defined by consequence, not building type. Three pillars: topology, machines, operations. And failure costs dwarf infrastructure costs, which funds everything. Next: the mathematics of availability.
