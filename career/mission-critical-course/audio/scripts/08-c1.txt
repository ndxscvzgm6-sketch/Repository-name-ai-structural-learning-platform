Lesson eight. Heat, and the chilled-water plant. Here's the physics that runs the second half of the facility: essentially every watt of electricity delivered to a computer becomes heat, continuously. A fifty-megawatt data center is a fifty-megawatt heater that must never be allowed to warm up. Cooling isn't an accessory to the power system; it's its equal twin.

Follow the heat out of the building, in a cascade. Hot air comes off the servers and hits cooling coils. The coils are filled with chilled water, which carries the heat to the central plant. There, chillers, big refrigeration machines, pump the heat out of the chilled water loop and into a second loop, the condenser water loop. And that loop carries it to cooling towers outside, which throw it to the sky, mostly by evaporation. Room air, to chilled water, to refrigerant, to condenser water, to atmosphere. Five hand-offs, every second, forever.

Learn one distinction cold, because it's a shibboleth: CRAC versus CRAH. A CRAC, with a C, is a computer room air conditioner: it has its own Compressor, a self-contained refrigeration unit. A CRAH, with an H, is a computer room air Handler: just a fan and a chilled-water coil, no compressor, the cooling is made centrally by the chillers. Big facilities standardize on CRAHs plus a central plant, because refrigeration is far more efficient in a few big machines than a hundred small ones. C for compressor, H for hydronic. That one letter tells insiders you know the plant.

Scale and plumbing. Data center chillers come in ranks, commonly five-hundred-ton units in an N-plus-one lineup. The water math is worth owning: each ton of cooling needs about two point four gallons per minute of chilled water. So a five-thousand-ton plant circulates twelve thousand gallons a minute through pipes two feet or more in diameter. And water is heavy: a twenty-four-inch main carries about two hundred pounds of water per foot of pipe, before you count the steel. Pumping comes in two philosophies: the classic primary-secondary scheme, a constant loop through the chillers decoupled from a variable loop to the loads, and the modern variable-primary scheme, fewer pumps, smarter controls. Either way, pumps are N-plus-one, headered so any pump can serve.

And here's the mindset transfer from the electrical lessons: draw the water one-line and hunt the single points of failure exactly the same way. Redundant chillers mean nothing if all of them drink make-up water from one city connection through one backflow preventer. Redundant pumps mean nothing on a single header with no isolation valves. The plant deserves the same paranoia as the switchgear.

For the structural engineer, the plant is your heaviest tenant. Chillers are tens of tons each, filled with water, always design for operating weight, never shipping weight. Pump skids sit on inertia bases to tame vibration. Piping galleries hang filled weight from your structure with seismic bracing, and big pipes shove real thrust forces into anchors at every bend. When the mechanical engineer moves a chiller, your building changes. Be in that meeting.

Takeaways: every watt in is a watt out through five hand-offs. CRAC has the compressor, CRAH is the coil. Two point four gallons per minute per ton, and water is structure-crushingly heavy.
