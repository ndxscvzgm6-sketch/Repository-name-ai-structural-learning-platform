Lesson four. Reading the one-line diagram. Every electrical conversation in this industry happens over one drawing: the single-line, or one-line, diagram. It compresses the whole electrical system onto one page: sources, transformations, protection, and paths. When an interviewer slides a one-line across the table, they're testing whether you can walk the electron. So let's walk it.

Power arrives from the utility at high voltage and lands at the site substation, where big transformers, fifty to a hundred megavolt-amps, step it down to medium voltage, typically somewhere between eleven and thirty-three kilovolts. That medium voltage runs around the campus to lineups of medium-voltage switchgear: rows of circuit breakers and protective relays in dedicated rooms, distributing and protecting the feeds.

From there, unit transformers, roughly one for every two-and-a-half to three megawatts, step down to utilization voltage, usually four hundred eighty volts, three phase. Those feed the low-voltage switchgear: long walls of breakers pushing thousands of amps to everything downstream: the U P S systems, the mechanical plant, the building loads.

Behind the U P S, power flows to P D Us, power distribution units, which transform and split it into circuits for the server rows. A floor-standing P D U weighs five to ten thousand pounds, remember that when someone calls it just an electrical box. Modern halls then run the power overhead in busway, rigid aluminum or copper bus in a housing, with tap-off boxes dropping down to each rack. And at the very end, each rack has two power strips: an A feed and a B feed, coming from completely separate paths. That dual cord at the rack is the visible tip of the entire redundancy philosophy.

Now, how do you spot the redundancy on the drawing? Four things. Look for two utility sources, or a utility plus a generator plant tying in through paralleling gear. Look for the letters A and B repeated down both sides of the page, two of everything. Look for bus ties: breakers that connect the A board to the B board, so during maintenance one side can feed the other. And look for every I T load being fed twice. If you can point at the drawing and say, here's my path when this breaker is racked out for service, you've passed the literacy test.

One more thing to file for the future: for the biggest A I loads, the industry is experimenting past four-eighty-volt A C altogether, toward high-voltage D C distribution, because at a megawatt per rack, conventional wiring runs out of copper. You don't need to design it; you need to not look surprised when someone says it.

And the structural translation, because every block on that one-line lands in your world: substation transformers need massive pads with oil containment. Switchgear lineups are long, heavy, floor-anchored furniture with strict clearance zones around them. Battery rooms are some of the heaviest floors in the building. Busway hangs from your roof steel with seismic bracing. A one-line is secretly a structural loading map. Learn to read it as one.

Takeaways: utility, substation, medium voltage, unit transformers, four-eighty switchgear, U P S, P D U, busway, dual cords at the rack. Redundancy shows up as A and B, bus ties, and two feeds everywhere. Next: the U P S itself, the machine at the center of everything.
