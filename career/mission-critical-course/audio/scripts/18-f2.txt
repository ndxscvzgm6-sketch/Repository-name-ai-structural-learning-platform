Lesson eighteen. The design review, a simulation. You're the owner's engineer on Project Sable: a forty-eight megawatt, two-hall A I data center. Tilt-up shell, slab on grade, racks at a hundred thirty-five kilowatts on direct-to-chip liquid, N-plus-one generators, two-N U P S with lithium batteries, a three-to-make-two chilled water plant with thermal storage. The design-build team just issued sixty-percent documents. I'll show you four exhibits; pause after each and decide what you'd flag before I give the answers.

Exhibit A, the electrical one-line. It shows beautiful A and B systems in separate rooms. But trace the feeders: both leave the substation in a single shared duct bank for three hundred feet before splitting. And the two lithium battery rooms sit back to back, sharing a wall. What do you flag? The shared duct bank is a classic physical S P O F: one excavation, one fault, one flood defeats two N; require diverse routing with real separation. The back-to-back battery rooms undermine compartmentation: a thermal runaway event and its suppression shouldn't be able to touch both sides; demand rated separation, independent ventilation, and blast relief that doesn't cross-contaminate. And ask for the selective coordination study now, at sixty percent, not as a closeout formality.

Exhibit B, the mechanical schematic. The three-to-make-two plant is elegant. But all three systems draw make-up water from one municipal tap through one backflow preventer. And the thermal storage tank's discharge valve is a single motorized valve, fail closed on loss of signal. Flags? The single water connection is the S P O F hiding behind a redundant plant, dual connections or on-site storage. And that fail-closed valve is Singapore rebuilt on purpose: loss of a control signal locks away your thermal battery exactly when you need it. Require fail-open or fail-in-place with a manual override, plus a hardwired open command on loss of cooling.

Exhibit C, structural and equipment coordination. Fourteen air-cooled chillers sit over Hall Two behind a fourteen-foot screen wall; the generator pads carry a vendor's standard anchorage detail; the battery room slab says one hundred fifty pounds per square foot, per code. Flags, and this exhibit is yours: chillers over an operating hall demand operating weights, vibration isolation coordinated with roof stiffness, and removal paths, concurrent maintainability is physical. The screen wall is a serious wind element with snow drift behind it, whose calc? Vendor-standard anchorage is not site-specific seismic design at the highest importance level; require stamped anchorage calculations for every component. And a hundred fifty pounds per square foot for a battery room is likely wrong by two or three times, even for lithium; demand the rack point loads and design to actual.

Exhibit D, the schedule. The builder proposes compressing commissioning from ten weeks to five by overlapping functional failure testing with early server installation in Hall One. Flag? Failure testing over live customer equipment is how test scripts become real outages. Offer alternatives, phase the commissioning by hall, add shifts, but protect the pull-the-plug test absolutely. Schedule pressure is normal; trading commissioning integrity is not. And notice the owner's engineer's style: a constructive no, with options.

Now keep the reusable question set: What's the basis of design, in writing? Where do the A and B paths share a room, a route, a controller, or a human? What's every device's fail-safe position, and then what happens? What are the operating weights, anchorage calcs, and removal paths? And is the proof current, coordination study, commissioning plan, capacity trends? Ten questions, any package, any vendor. That's the job. Next, the capstone: your ninety-second command of the whole discipline.
