Lesson six. Generators and fuel systems. When the grid disappears for hours or days, the facility becomes its own power plant. Diesel engine-generators, with natural gas growing, are the long-duration layer beneath the U P S's few minutes.

The scale first, because it surprises people. Individual units cluster around two to two and a half megawatts, and campuses deploy them in ranks. Amazon's site in Manassas, Virginia runs ninety-three generators at two and a half megawatts each. The state of Virginia alone has more than ten thousand permitted data center generators, twenty-seven gigawatts of standby capacity, a shadow power fleet the size of a small country's grid. And redundancy applies here too: fleets carry spares, or share reserve units across blocks.

Now the drama of the first sixty seconds. Time zero: the utility fails. The U P S carries the computers without a flicker. Two seconds in: the controls confirm this is a real outage, not a blink, and signal all engines to start. By ten to fifteen seconds: engines have cranked, fired, and reached speed, hospital codes actually require ten seconds to load for life safety. Then the choreography: paralleling switchgear synchronizes the engines to one another, matching voltage, frequency, and phase, before closing onto the bus. And the load transfers in stages, big mechanical loads deliberately delayed a few seconds so a wall of motors doesn't slam the engines. Sixty seconds after the lights blinked in the neighborhood, the campus hums along on its own power. And when the utility returns, the transfer back is engineered and drilled with equal care, remember British Airways.

Fuel is a system, not a tank. Each engine has a day tank nearby holding hours of fuel, automatically refilled from bulk storage: big site tanks holding a campus target of twenty-four to forty-eight hours at full load, some owners spec ninety-six. In between: redundant transfer pumps, filtration, and fuel polishing, because stored diesel literally grows contamination and absorbs water; degraded fuel is a real outage cause. Environmental rules pile on: spill containment berms above thirteen hundred twenty gallons, air permits capping run hours.

And here's the case that made fuel systems famous. Superstorm Sandy, New York, twenty twelve. Multiple facilities had their generators running high and dry above the flood, and failed anyway, because the fuel pumps and day tanks feeding those engines were in flooded basements. N Y U hospital evacuated patients down dark stairwells. One Manhattan data center survived on a human bucket brigade hauling diesel up seventeen flights. The lesson, which is now a standard review: a backup system's real flood elevation is the elevation of its lowest critical component. Trace the whole chain, engine, tanks, pumps, controls, vents, against the flood maps. That's simultaneously a reliability review and a structural siting review, and it's yours.

For the structural engineer: engine skids of twenty to thirty-five tons on pads sized by the old rule of about twice the machine's mass to tame vibration. Exhaust stacks and radiators catching wind. Bulk tanks, a fifty-thousand gallon tank holds three hundred fifty thousand pounds of fuel, with containment berms, and buried tanks that want to float in high groundwater. All of it anchored to the highest seismic standard, because backup power must survive the very event it exists for.

Takeaways: two-and-a-half megawatt engines by the dozens. Ten seconds to start, a minute to full choreography. Twenty-four to forty-eight hours of fuel, kept clean and above the flood line. And Sandy's law: the lowest critical component sets the system's real elevation. Next: transfer switches, protection, and failing over gracefully.
