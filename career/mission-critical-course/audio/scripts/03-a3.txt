Lesson three. Why facilities actually fail. Everything so far is theory. This is the scoreboard, from the industry's standard reference, the Uptime Institute's annual outage analysis. The results should reorganize your intuitions.

Start with the league table. The number one cause of serious outages, year after year, is power, about forty-five percent of impactful incidents. And within power, the single most common culprit is the U P S, the uninterruptible power supply. Sit with the irony: the machine whose only job is preventing outages is the leading cause of them. The protection layer is itself the biggest risk. That's not an argument against U P S systems; it's the argument for redundancy of the protection itself, for battery monitoring, and for treating maintenance on these systems with surgical seriousness.

Second place, and rising fast: I T and network problems, about a quarter of outages, mostly configuration and change-management errors. Third: cooling, less frequent than power but brutal at modern equipment densities, where a cooling pause becomes an outage within minutes. And then the rare catastrophic tail: fire and water.

Now the finding that should change how you think about engineering altogether. Nearly forty percent of organizations suffered a major outage caused by human error in the past three years. And of those human-error incidents, about eighty-five percent trace to procedures: staff not following them, or the procedures themselves being wrong. Let that land. The dominant failure mode of the most engineered buildings on Earth is: someone skipped a step. The industry's answer is the discipline you'll meet in the operations lessons: written procedures for everything, drills, change control, and designs that make the wrong action physically difficult.

Two case files to make it concrete, both worth retelling in interviews. Case one: British Airways, May twenty seventeen. During work at a data center near Heathrow, power was interrupted, and then, reportedly, reconnected improperly. The resulting surge damaged servers and crippled the airline's global operations over a holiday weekend: seventy-five thousand stranded passengers, tens of millions of pounds. The lesson isn't that power failed; power failures are expected. The lesson is that the recovery was the catastrophe. Restoration sequences must be engineered, written down, and drilled, and one of the sharpest questions you can ask in any design review is: who is authorized to re-energize this system, following which document?

Case two: O V H cloud, Strasbourg, France, March twenty twenty-one. A fire started in a U P S room, in a unit that had been extensively serviced that very morning, and it destroyed a five-story data center. Three and a half million websites went dark; some customer data was lost forever. The investigation found compounding factors: no automatic fire suppression in the U P S room, delays cutting electrical power, and a free-air cooling design that fed the flames. Layered lessons: maintenance windows are risk windows. The highest-energy rooms need the best detection and suppression. And airflow paths are fire paths, a structural and mechanical joint problem.

Takeaways. Power leads the league table, and the U P S leads power. Human and procedural failures run through the majority of incidents, eighty-five percent of them procedure-shaped. And the two stories, B A and O V H, teach the two deepest morals: recovery is a designed procedure, and the protection systems themselves need protecting.
