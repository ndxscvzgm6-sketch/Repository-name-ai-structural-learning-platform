Lesson seventeen. The case files. This industry is unusually shaped by named disasters, its shared scar tissue, and citing them fluently is how you sound like you've been here for years. Six dossiers, each with its permanent lesson. Details as publicly reported, and say "reportedly" in interviews too; it signals discipline.

Dossier one. Delta Air Lines, Atlanta, August twenty sixteen. A failure in aging power control equipment, reportedly involving a fire in transfer switchgear, cascaded until the airline's global operations collapsed for days: two thousand cancelled flights, about a hundred fifty million dollars. The post-incident revelation: a meaningful share of supposedly dual-fed equipment turned out to be connected to only one power path. The lesson: a two-N diagram is only as real as its worst-audited corner. Redundancy claims decay over years of small changes, which is why the S P O F hunt is a recurring audit, not a design-phase memory.

Dossier two. British Airways, May twenty seventeen. Power interrupted, then reportedly restored incorrectly; the surge did the damage: seventy-five thousand passengers stranded. Lesson: recovery is a procedure, energization sequences are engineered, written, authorized, and drilled. Who may reconnect power, per which document, is a design review question.

Dossier three. O V H cloud, Strasbourg, March twenty twenty-one. Fire seated in a U P S room, one unit serviced that morning, destroyed a five-story data center: three and a half million websites dark, some data unrecoverable. No automatic suppression in that room; airflow design fed the spread. Lessons: maintenance windows are risk windows, the highest-energy rooms deserve the best protection, and airflow paths are fire paths.

Dossier four. Superstorm Sandy, New York, twenty twelve. Generators above the flood, starved by fuel pumps and day tanks below it; a hospital evacuated by stairwell, a data center saved by a bucket brigade. Lesson: a system's real flood rating is its lowest critical component, trace every dependency chain against hazard elevations. Now a standard siting review, and substantially a structural one.

Dossier five. Equinix, Singapore, October twenty twenty-three. A contractor's wrong signal closed the chilled-water buffer-tank valves during a planned upgrade; two national banks lost the better part of two days, two and a half million transactions failed, with power available throughout. Lessons: controls are common-mode, fail-safe positions matter, change management binds third parties too, and at modern density, thermal margins are minutes.

Dossier six. Winter storm Uri, Texas, February twenty twenty-one. The grid itself failed for days in single-digit cold. Facilities with fuel autonomy, winterized equipment, and disciplined load management rode through; others learned that utility redundancy within one grid is still one dependency. Lessons: regional common-mode events are real, hence twenty-four to forty-eight hour fuel targets, winterization, and, for the biggest operators, geographic redundancy across grids entirely.

Use these in interviews with a signature move: pair the incident with the design response you would own. After Sandy, I'd audit every fuel-chain elevation. After Singapore, I'd walk every valve's fail-safe position. After Delta, I'd re-verify dual-cording on a sampling basis every year. Incident plus owned response equals fluency.

Takeaways: six names, Delta, B A, O V H, Sandy, Singapore, Uri, and six laws: audit the claims, script the recovery, protect the protectors, respect the waterline, distrust the controls, and plan past the fence line.
