Lesson thirteen. Controls, monitoring, and security. Thousands of machines, chillers, valves, breakers, fans, act as one facility because a control layer conducts them. And that layer is increasingly where outages are born: remember Singapore, where the fatal event was a signal, not a broken machine.

Three systems share the nervous system, and knowing which is which is basic fluency. The B M S, building management system, runs the mechanical choreography: chiller staging, valve positions, fan speeds, alarms. The E P M S, electrical power monitoring system, watches the electrical side: metering every bus, capturing waveforms at millisecond resolution so that after any event you can reconstruct exactly what happened, in what order, the facility's flight recorder. And D C I M, data center infrastructure management, bridges facilities and I T: which rack has power and cooling headroom, where the next hundred servers can land.

Everything automated obeys a written script called the sequence of operations. On utility loss: confirm for two seconds, start engines, shed these loads, stage those pumps. Commissioning exists largely to prove those sequences, and post-incident reviews usually discover reality diverged from the script, or the script was wrong. Owner's engineers read sequences of operations the way lawyers read contracts, hunting the corner cases: what happens if two things fail at once? What if the sensor lies?

Now the hard lesson of the era: controls are the ultimate common-mode failure. You can build two magnificent independent cooling paths, and if one controller commands both, you have one system with extra steps. The defenses, and this is a checklist worth memorizing: segment the controls as strictly as the power, A-side controllers for A-side equipment. Define fail-safe positions for every device, what does this valve do on loss of signal? Singapore's buffer-tank valves failed closed, locking away the thermal battery; between stored cooling and the load, the answer must be fail open. Keep manual overrides at the equipment, so a human with a hand-wheel can defeat a confused computer. Hardwire the life-safety trips independent of any network. And treat the control network itself as an attack surface: operational technology cybersecurity, segmented from the corporate network, because a compromised B M S is a remote-controlled outage.

Physical security, briefly, because it rhymes: defense in layers. Site perimeter with berms and anti-ram barriers, often doubling as your screen walls. Hardened shell. The mantrap lobby, interlocked double doors defeating tailgating. Badge plus biometric at the hall. Locked racks inside. Each layer buys time and evidence. The same redundancy philosophy, applied to intrusion.

For the structural engineer: control rooms want sight lines and their own hardening. Anti-ram perimeter elements are structural design. And your deepest contribution is a design philosophy: assume the controls will someday command the wrong thing, and prefer physics over software, gravity drains, spring-return valves, mechanical interlocks. The best fail-safe is one that doesn't need electricity to be right.

Takeaways: B M S runs mechanical, E P M S watches electrical, D C I M bridges to I T. Sequences of operations are the facility's script, and reviewers read them adversarially. Controls are common-mode failure incarnate: segment them, fail-safe them, override them by hand. And security is redundancy against humans.
